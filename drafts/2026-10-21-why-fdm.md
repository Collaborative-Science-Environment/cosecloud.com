title: Why the SciSpinner Max is 3D printed
date: 2026-10-21
author: Jerry Miao
tag: engineering
summary: The frame, core and housings of the SciSpinner Max are printed in PETG and TPU rather than moulded or machined. Here is why, and where we deliberately do not use printed parts.

The structure of a SciSpinner Max, the base, frame, core, housings and sample holders, is made by FDM 3D printing, in PETG for the rigid parts and TPU for gaskets and the shipping inserts. Bearings, shafts and fasteners are metal. People sometimes ask whether that is a compromise. For an instrument made in small numbers and often to order, we think it is the right process, for five reasons.

**Volume.** Injection moulding is the cheapest way to make a plastic part once a mould exists, but each mould costs thousands of dollars and only pays for itself over thousands of parts. We build tens of instruments a year, each with dozens of different parts. Machining every part from billet would cost several times as much and take longer. Printing has no tooling cost, so the hundredth part costs the same as the first.

**Variants.** Many of our instruments are not quite standard: a chamber modified for a particular sample, a core with a pump and media bag, a holder for a specific plate or flask. With printing, a custom part is a CAD change and a print, not a new mould. The blank sample holder we ship can be drilled, machined or reprinted by the customer to suit their own samples.

**Revisions.** When we improve a part, the next instrument gets it. There is no stock of old mouldings to use up first, and no tooling to scrap. The current design is the result of a great many small changes since [the 2018 prototype](/posts/second-prototype), most of which would not have been worth making if each had needed a new mould.

**Spares.** Because every part exists as a file, we can make a replacement for any SciSpinner we have shipped, including the earliest ones, without holding inventory. Most repairs are a reprinted part in the post.

**Material.** PETG is tough rather than brittle, absorbs little moisture, and softens at around 80 °C, far above the 37 °C of a cell-culture incubator. TPU stays flexible for seals. Both cope with the warm, humid air an instrument lives in for weeks at a time.

There are limits, and the design works around them. Printed parts have layer lines and looser tolerances than machined ones, so everything that sets the accuracy of the motion is metal: the bearings, the shafts and the motor mounts that hold them in line. Printed seats are sized to hold the bearings, not to define the axes. And PETG cannot be autoclaved, so the parts that touch samples are made to be wiped down or replaced rather than sterilised by heat.
